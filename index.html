    ABOUT

    The day's headlines, and the narratives that run through them.

    The Present Tense gathers hundreds of headlines from publishers in dozens of countries and lays them out as a field, roughly by geography, each one a link to the original report. Once a day a language model reads the whole collection and writes, in plain language, five narratives. A narrative is an idea that joins unlike events: a price in one country, a strike in a second, a court case in a third, that turn out to share a cause, a mechanism, an actor or a shape.

    The narrative is the idea; the headlines are its evidence; what it joins is listed at the top of each one. They are shown along the bottom of the page. Every edition is kept: lists where we were, each narrative says what it continues from the edition before and what has changed, and the field can be stepped back to the headlines an earlier edition read. ≋Threads draws the narratives across days and carries the Chronicle, a running account of where things stand by these headlines, revised after each edition. ¶Lineage says where the idea comes from.

    is a second register beside the headlines: yesterday's most-viewed Wikipedia articles in each of twenty-eight language editions, Bluesky's trending topics and the hashtags trending on a few Mastodon servers. It shows what people looked up and talked about against what publishers printed. It is context for the narratives, never evidence, and the model may mention it but cannot cite it.

    The narratives are interpretations, not findings. Each one states a claim, shows which headlines it rests on, says what might connect them, and then sets out the strongest case against itself in a final section called The case against. Read both. The point is the argument, not the verdict.

    What you are looking at

    Headlines come from the GDELT Article List, which indexes world news metadata: titles, links, publishers, languages and timestamps. Nothing else is copied. Each headline links to its publisher. The map places a headline at its publisher's country, never at the event's location. Headlines appear in English by default; the original wording is always one tap away (文Original in the header), and a country's press is not that country's people. is every headline in the edition as a plain, searchable list.

    Geography on this page is loose on purpose. Headlines are pushed apart so you can read them, so Europe swells and the oceans fill. Hollow rings mark countries we have publishers for but heard nothing from in this collection. That is a gap in our listening, not a silence in the country. Many countries have no publisher here at all. The field is partial and says so.

    How we got here

    Every narrative ends with four horizons, each anchored to the start of a regime the world is still living inside: since 2020, since 2008, since 1989 and since 1945. Each asks what that regime put in place that today's headlines are living inside. When a join is about something older than 1945, water, land, a border, a religion, the writer may add a fifth horizon that reaches further back and names its own starting year. They place the day's pattern on longer trajectories, sociological, economic, demographic, migratory, environmental, technological and legal, and they point to the moments and books they stand on. A ruler above them marks those moments. They are written as a trajectory, not a verdict: how the present might have arrived, with the dates and institutions named so the reader can check and disagree.

    The tide

    compares every collection with the one before. Headlines that arrived, stayed or departed are marked, and departed headlines can be shown as ghosts. The present is only legible against the recent past.

    How it stays live

    A scheduled job collects a fresh bounded sample of headlines, translates new ones, and asks Claude to write a new edition of narratives against a strict schema: a claim, sourced sections, a mandatory case against, and four historical horizons. Headlines are collected four times a day and read once, in a batch request that runs the same model at half the price; the headlines a narrative cites stay in the field until the next edition. The site refuses an edition whose citations do not resolve. Collection without a key keeps the previous narratives and marks them as older than the headlines.

    What it costs, and how to help

    The site is free, with no paywall and no advertising. It costs about US$1.15 a day to run, almost all of it the model's fees for writing the narratives and revising the Chronicle. If you read it and want it to keep going, you can buy it a coffee; a coffee covers two or three editions. Donations pay that bill and nothing else.

    Write to hello@presenttense.news. If a narrative joins things it should not, or misses a join it should have made, that is the kind of reading the site is for, and it is worth saying so.
