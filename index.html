    ABOUT

    The day's headlines, and the narratives that run through them.

    The Present Tense gathers hundreds of headlines from publishers in dozens of countries and lays them out as a field, roughly by geography, each one a link to the original report. Once a day a language model reads the whole collection and describes, in plain language, five narratives that connect headlines across countries: a story in Lagos with one in Taipei with one in Córdoba. They are shown along the bottom of the page.

    The narratives are interpretations, not findings. Each one states a claim, shows which headlines it rests on, says what might connect them, and then sets out the strongest case against itself in a final section called The case against. Read both. The point is the argument, not the verdict.

    What you are looking at

    Headlines come from the GDELT Article List, which indexes world news metadata: titles, links, publishers, languages and timestamps. Nothing else is copied. Each headline links to its publisher. The map places a headline at its publisher's country, never at the event's location. Headlines appear in English by default; the original wording is always one tap away, and a country's press is not that country's people.

    Geography on this page is loose on purpose. Headlines are pushed apart so you can read them, so Europe swells and the oceans fill. Hollow rings mark countries we have publishers for but heard nothing from in this collection. That is a gap in our listening, not a silence in the country. Many countries have no publisher here at all. The field is partial and says so.

    How we got here

    Every narrative ends with four horizons: the past decade, the past fifty years, since September 2001 and since 1945. They place the day's pattern on longer trajectories, sociological, economic, demographic, migratory, environmental, technological and legal, and they point to the moments and books they stand on. A ruler above them marks those moments. They are written as a trajectory, not a verdict: how the present might have arrived, with the dates and institutions named so the reader can check and disagree.

    The tide

    Every edition is compared with the one before. Headlines that arrived, stayed or departed are marked, and departed headlines can be shown as ghosts. The present is only legible against the recent past.

    How it stays live

    A scheduled job collects a fresh bounded sample of headlines, translates new ones, and asks Claude to write a new edition of narratives against a strict schema: a claim, sourced sections, a mandatory case against, and four historical horizons. Headlines are collected four times a day and read once, in a batch request that runs the same model at half the price; the headlines a narrative cites stay in the field until the next edition. The site refuses an edition whose citations do not resolve. Collection without a key keeps the previous narratives and marks them as older than the headlines.
